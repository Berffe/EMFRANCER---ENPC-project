##### OVERALL VERSION #############################################################################
	Once the landing decision is given to the RPi, we define 3 regimes of altitude: - still far from landing, but already started landing sequence; - short final, where the landing geometry mask is fully grown; - immediate landing, too close to the ground for the camera to reliably identify the terrain (turn it off and finish landing); The landing mask in question is also divided in 3 zones that grow with laws still to be detailed. The zones will be: - zone 0: frame zones that do not matter for the landing zone; - zone 1: cautious zone, 'too close for comfort' zone; - zone 2: critical zone, if we confidently detect objects here, we abort immediately. The actual functions to define an object detection (with time windows guaranteeing robustness and stability), the threshold for each abortion (zone 1 and 2) are still to be defined and refined based on tests.

	The landing decision logic is based on an altitude-dependent visual risk assessment rather than on instantaneous obstacle detections. First, the aircraft altitude is smoothed so that noisy telemetry does not cause unstable transitions between operational regimes. This smoothed altitude is then used to determine the current landing regime: initial final approach, short final, or immediate landing. In the first two regimes, the camera image is partitioned into altitude-dependent spatial zones that evolve through scaling and translation as the aircraft descends. Zone 0 is irrelevant to the landing decision, Zone 1 is a caution region, and Zone 2 is the critical landing corridor.

	At each inference step, every detection contributes to a frame-level risk according to a weighted function of its zone, confidence, class, and optionally its apparent size. Detections in Zone 2 are penalized more heavily than those in Zone 1. These frame-level risks are then accumulated over a temporal observation window containing the last N inference steps, with more recent observations given higher weight. This temporal accumulation makes the decision robust to isolated false positives, transient misses, and detection flicker. In addition to this continuous risk score, hard safety rules are defined for particularly dangerous situations, such as persistent high-confidence detections in Zone 2.

	The final landing command is obtained through a hysteretic decision rule. Two thresholds are used: an abort threshold and a clear threshold. If the accumulated risk exceeds the abort threshold, landing is rejected and an abort maneuver is commanded. If the accumulated risk remains below the clear threshold for long enough, landing is permitted. If the risk remains between both thresholds, the previous decision is maintained. This hysteresis prevents unstable oscillations between landing permission and landing rejection. Finally, once the aircraft reaches the immediate landing regime, the visual risk assessment is disabled and the landing is completed under the nominal terminal behavior of the flight controller.

##### FORMAL VERSION ##############################################################################
	Once authority over the landing decision is transferred from the autopilot mission logic to the Raspberry Pi companion computer, the visual landing-assessment module operates according to three altitude-dependent regimes.

	In the first regime, corresponding to the initial assessment phase of the final approach, the aircraft has already entered the landing sequence but remains sufficiently far from touchdown for visual information to be both relevant and exploitable. In this phase, the objective is to monitor the prospective landing corridor and identify obstacles that may compromise the continuation of the approach. Because the aircraft is still relatively distant from the ground, the region of greatest operational relevance occupies only a limited portion of the image.

	In the second regime, corresponding to short final, the aircraft is close enough to the ground that the full landing geometry must be considered. At this stage, the image mask used for risk assessment reaches its maximum operational extent. The purpose of this regime is to evaluate, with increased strictness, whether the touchdown corridor and its immediate surroundings remain free of relevant obstacles. This phase is the most critical for the visual decision process, since it directly supports the binary choice between continuing the landing or commanding an abort maneuver.

	In the third regime, corresponding to immediate landing, the aircraft is considered too close to the ground for the visual system to provide sufficiently reliable or timely terrain assessment. At such low altitude, the remaining time-to-impact is too short for robust obstacle discrimination and for a safe maneuver reversal based solely on newly acquired visual information. Consequently, the perception-driven assessment is disabled, and the aircraft is allowed to complete the landing under the nominal terminal guidance of the flight controller.

	To support these three regimes, the image is partitioned into three altitude-dependent spatial zones. These zones are defined through a landing mask whose geometry evolves as a function of altitude.

	Zone 0 designates the regions of the image considered irrelevant for the landing decision. Detections falling exclusively within this zone do not influence the landing status.

	Zone 1 designates a cautionary region surrounding the most probable landing corridor. Obstacles detected in this zone do not necessarily imply immediate rejection of the landing, but they increase the level of concern and may lead to an abort decision if they persist over time, appear in sufficient number, or are associated with high confidence.

	Zone 2 designates the critical landing corridor. It corresponds to the image region judged most likely to contain the immediate touchdown path and the ground area required for safe landing continuation. Detections occurring in this zone are treated with the highest severity. In particular, sufficiently confident and temporally persistent detections within Zone 2 trigger immediate landing rejection and the command of an abort maneuver.

	The geometric evolution of Zones 1 and 2 is altitude-dependent. At higher altitude, the critical zone is relatively small and concentrated in the portion of the image corresponding to the projected landing path ahead of the aircraft. As altitude decreases, the mask expands to reflect the increasing relevance of the near-ground portion of the scene. At short final, the mask reaches its maximum extent, such that the full operational landing corridor is considered.

	Since single-frame detections are not sufficiently reliable for flight-critical decisions, obstacle assessment is not performed on an instantaneous basis. Instead, detections are evaluated over temporal windows in order to ensure robustness against transient false positives, missed detections, and short-lived image artifacts. The exact persistence criteria, confidence thresholds, and zone-dependent rejection rules are left as design parameters to be refined experimentally. In particular, the following elements must be calibrated through testing: the altitude thresholds separating the three operational regimes, the growth laws governing the geometry of Zones 1 and 2, the minimum temporal persistence required for a detection to be considered valid, and the rejection thresholds associated with each zone.

	Under this framework, the landing decision is no longer based solely on the presence or absence of detections in a single frame, but on a structured, altitude-aware, and temporally filtered assessment of obstacle risk along the projected landing corridor.

	The proposed landing-decision strategy is formulated as an altitude-aware and temporally robust visual risk-assessment process. Its purpose is to determine whether the final landing phase may safely continue or whether an abort maneuver must be commanded. The logic is intentionally designed to avoid reliance on single-frame detections, since such detections are inherently sensitive to noise, missed observations, and transient artifacts.

	The first component of the method is the smoothing of altitude telemetry. Rather than using raw altitude measurements directly, a filtered altitude estimate is employed in order to reduce the effect of sensor noise and prevent unstable regime transitions. This smoothed altitude is used to determine the current operational regime of the landing sequence. Three regimes are considered: an initial final-approach regime, in which the aircraft remains sufficiently high for partial visual corridor assessment; a short-final regime, in which the full landing geometry is considered; and an immediate-landing regime, in which the aircraft is considered too close to the ground for reliable visual reassessment and the visual decision layer is therefore deactivated.

	For the initial final-approach and short-final regimes, the image is partitioned into altitude-dependent spatial zones through a landing mask whose geometry evolves as a function of altitude. Zone 0 corresponds to image regions that are irrelevant to the landing decision. Zone 1 corresponds to a cautionary region, representing areas that are close enough to the projected landing corridor to justify concern but not necessarily immediate rejection. Zone 2 represents the critical landing corridor and corresponds to the image region most directly associated with the immediate touchdown path. The geometry of Zones 1 and 2 evolves through scaling and translation operations as altitude decreases, thereby reflecting the increasing relevance of near-ground image regions during descent.

	At each inference step, the set of detections provided by the perception module is converted into a frame-level risk measure. Each detection contributes to this measure through a weighted function that depends on its spatial zone, confidence level, semantic class, and, if required, apparent image size. Zone-dependent weighting ensures that detections within the critical corridor contribute more strongly to risk than detections within the cautionary corridor. In this way, the method distinguishes between severe threats located directly on the touchdown path and less critical objects located in adjacent but still relevant areas.

	Since the landing decision must be robust to temporal inconsistency in detection outputs, frame-level risks are not used directly. Instead, they are accumulated over a finite observation window comprising the most recent N inference steps. More recent observations are assigned greater weight than older ones, thereby ensuring that persistent recent hazards dominate the assessment while still preserving a short-term memory of earlier detections. In parallel, hard rejection conditions may be defined for particularly hazardous cases, such as repeated high-confidence detections inside Zone 2. This dual structure combines a continuous notion of accumulated risk with explicit safety-trigger rules.

	The final decision is obtained through a hysteretic thresholding mechanism. Two distinct thresholds are defined: an abort threshold and a clear threshold, with the clear threshold strictly lower than the abort threshold. If the accumulated risk exceeds the abort threshold, the landing is rejected and an abort maneuver is commanded. If the accumulated risk remains below the clear threshold for a sufficient duration, landing is permitted. If the accumulated risk lies between these two thresholds, the previously issued decision is maintained. This hysteresis prevents unstable oscillations between landing acceptance and landing rejection when the risk measure fluctuates around a single threshold value.

	Overall, the proposed method may be interpreted as a three-layer process: altitude smoothing defines the relevant operational regime; regime-dependent image zoning defines where detections matter and with what severity; and temporally accumulated, hysteretically filtered risk determines whether landing should be allowed or aborted. This structure provides a principled and modular basis for the implementation of a robust visual landing supervisor.
	
	During a valid landing attempt, if the visual supervisor classifies the approach as safe, the companion computer issues no additional MAVLink command and allows the ArduPilot AUTO mission to continue its nominal NAV_LAND execution. Conversely, if the visual supervisor classifies the approach as unsafe while the aircraft is executing NAV_LAND, the companion computer issues a single MAV_CMD_DO_GO_AROUND command. ArduPilot then executes its native autolanding abort behavior, climbs to the configured safe altitude, and resumes the mission according to the abort-resume policy. In the proposed mission structure, this resume logic is defined through DO_LAND_START, thereby causing the aircraft to re-enter the landing pattern automatically and attempt a new landing sequence without operator intervention.
